Why use a gravity assist?

Rockets are limited by launch energy (C3). A flyby lets a planet do the work: relative to the planet the spacecraft leaves as fast as it arrived, but its direction is rotated. Because the planet is moving around the Sun, that rotation changes the spacecraft’s speed and direction relative to the Sun, for no fuel. The planet’s orbit changes by an unmeasurable amount.

Venus: cheaper launch. A short, cheap Earth→Venus leg plus a Venus swing-by points us at Halley. Launch C3 drops from about 22 to about 13 km²/s², so a heavier spacecraft fits on the same rocket. The price is a faster, later encounter.

Jupiter: gentler encounter. Halley orbits backwards, so a direct flyby is a head-on pass at about 67 km/s (Giotto in 1986 was around 68). Routing through Jupiter roughly halves that speed, which means sharper images and a longer look. The price is a much higher launch C3 (about 90) and a trip of almost five years.

Caveats. Planets use simple Keplerian elements, flybys are patched conics (the speed mismatch is the fuel a real burn would need), and Halley is two-body. This is a design study, not a flight plan.
